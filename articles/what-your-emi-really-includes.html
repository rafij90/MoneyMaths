What Your EMI Really Includes (It's Not What Most People Think)

What Your EMI Really Includes

Most borrowers know their EMI — the fixed monthly amount they pay. Very few know what that number is actually made of, or that its composition quietly changes every single month of the loan. Understanding this is the difference between a loan you manage well and one that manages you.

The two parts hiding inside every EMI

Every EMI (Equated Monthly Installment) is made of two components:

  • Principal — the actual amount you borrowed, being repaid
  • Interest — the cost of borrowing, charged on whatever principal is still outstanding

The EMI amount stays fixed for the life of the loan, but the split between these two doesn't. Early on, most of your EMI goes toward interest. Only in the later years does the balance shift toward principal.

Why early EMIs are mostly interest

Interest is calculated on the outstanding loan balance. At the very start of a loan, your outstanding balance is at its highest — so the interest portion is at its largest.

Take a ₹30,00,000 home loan at 9% annual interest for 20 years. The EMI works out to roughly ₹26,992/month. Here's how that EMI splits in different years:

Year Interest portion Principal portion
Year 1 ~₹2,68,000 ~₹56,000
Year 5 ~₹2,44,000 ~₹80,000
Year 10 ~₹1,97,000 ~₹1,27,000
Year 15 ~₹1,24,000 ~₹2,00,000
Year 20 ~₹20,000 ~₹3,04,000

In year 1, roughly 83% of what you pay is interest. It isn't until around year 12–13 that the split crosses over and principal starts dominating. This is why, after paying EMIs for 5 years on a 20-year loan, your outstanding loan amount has barely moved — even though you've paid a huge amount of money.

This single fact surprises more borrowers than almost anything else about loans. You can see your own loan's exact split using the EMI calculator.

Why this matters when you prepay

Because interest eats the early years, any extra payment you make early in the loan saves you far more than the same payment made later — because it removes principal at the point where it would otherwise have generated the most interest over the remaining tenure.

A ₹1,00,000 prepayment in year 2 of the loan above could save significantly more in total interest than the same ₹1,00,000 prepaid in year 15 — even though it's the exact same amount of money. When you prepay matters almost as much as how much you prepay.

Why refinancing later matters less than people think

Some borrowers wait years, hoping interest rates will drop, before refinancing. But if most of the early years were already spent paying interest, waiting means missing the window where prepayment or refinancing has the biggest impact. Checking your loan's amortization schedule early — even just once, at the start — helps you decide if an early lump-sum payment makes sense for you.

Three things this changes about how you should borrow

  1. A shorter tenure often beats a lower EMI. A shorter loan has a smaller total interest bill, even if the EMI is higher, because less time is spent in the high-interest early years.
  2. Any extra payment matters more early. If you expect a bonus or windfall, using part of it to prepay a loan in its first few years does more than the same amount later.
  3. "Total interest paid" tells you more than the EMI number. Two loans can have similar EMIs but very different total interest costs depending on tenure and rate — always compare the total cost, not just the monthly figure.

Quick FAQ

Does the EMI amount ever change? For fixed-rate loans, no — it stays the same for the full tenure. For floating-rate loans, the EMI (or the tenure) can change if the interest rate changes.

If I prepay, does my EMI go down or my tenure go down? Usually you get to choose. Reducing tenure (keeping EMI the same) saves more total interest than reducing the EMI (keeping tenure the same), because the loan closes sooner.

How can I see exactly how my own loan splits each year? Use the EMI calculator to generate your loan's amortization schedule and see the interest-vs-principal split for every year of your tenure.
