Imagine you buy a house with a bank loan today, but five years later, interest rates across the country fall. Your friend gets a new loan for the same type of house at a much lower rate. Both of you hold home loans, but the actual cost of borrowing is completely different for each of you. 

This same idea applies to big companies when they raise money by issuing bonds. 

## The core idea in one sentence

> **Cost of debt reflects the effective interest rate a company pays on its current borrowings after tax benefits, while Yield to Maturity (YTM) is the total annual return an investor expects if they buy a bond at its current market price and hold it until it matures.**

## Understanding the historical Cost of Debt

When a company needs money to build a new factory or expand its business, it can borrow from a bank or issue bonds to the public. The rate of interest promised on these loans or bonds is set at the time of borrowing. This is often called the coupon rate or historical cost of debt.

For example, if a company issued a bond five years ago promising to pay 9% interest every year, that 9% is its locked-in historical borrowing rate. The company must keep paying this 9% to its existing bondholders, no matter what happens to the economy later.

Because interest paid on business debt reduces the company's taxable income, the actual cost to the company is even lower. This reduced rate is known as the after-tax cost of debt.

## What is Yield to Maturity (YTM)?

While the company pays a fixed interest rate based on the old contract, the bond itself gets traded on the stock market every day. Its price goes up and down based on current interest rates, inflation, and the financial health of the company.

Yield to Maturity, or YTM, looks at the bond from the perspective of a new buyer today. It calculates the total return you will get if you buy the bond at today's market price, collect all the remaining interest payments, and get the final face value back when the bond matures. 

Because market prices change every day, the YTM changes every day too. It represents the current market rate for that company's debt.

## Why the two rates are different

The main reason these two rates differ is time and market movement. The historical borrowing rate is a backward-looking number set in the past. YTM is a forward-looking number based on today's market conditions.

If general interest rates in India go up, old bonds paying lower interest become less attractive. Their market prices drop. When the market price of a bond drops below its original face value, its YTM goes up.

Conversely, if the company's credit rating improves, investors feel safer. They are willing to pay a higher price for the bond today, which drives the YTM down. 

Therefore, if a company wants to raise fresh money from the market today, it cannot rely on its old historical borrowing rate. It must offer a rate close to the current YTM to attract new investors.

## A simple numerical example

Let us look at a practical example using an Indian corporate bond to see how the math works.

Suppose a company issued a bond with a face value of Rs 1,000 three years ago. The promised interest rate was 8% per year. The company pays Rs 80 as interest every year. 

Today, market interest rates have increased, so similar new bonds are offering higher returns. Because of this, the market price of our company's old bond drops to Rs 950.

Here is how the numbers look for the company versus a new investor:

| Feature | Historical Perspective (Company) | Current Market Perspective (New Investor) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Relevant Metric** | Historical Coupon Rate | Yield to Maturity (YTM) |
| **Bond Face Value** | Rs 1,000 | Rs 1,000 |
| **Current Market Price** | Rs 1,000 (At issuance) | Rs 950 |
| **Annual Interest Payment** | Rs 80 | Rs 80 |
| **Effective Annual Rate** | 8.00% | Higher than 8.00% (approx. 9.15% depending on years left) |
| **Main Driver** | Original contract signed in past | Today's buying price and market demand |

The company continues to pay Rs 80 on the original Rs 1,000 face value (an 8% rate). However, a new investor buys the bond for just Rs 950. That investor receives the same Rs 80 annual interest plus a profit of Rs 50 when the bond matures at Rs 1,000. Because the investor paid less upfront, their YTM is significantly higher than 8%.

If this company decides to issue brand-new bonds today, it will have to pay close to the higher YTM rate to attract buyers, making its new cost of debt higher than its historical cost of debt.

## What this means for you

* **For company analysis:** Always look at YTM if you want to know what it costs a company to raise new money today, rather than what it paid in the past.
* **For bond investors:** A bond's promised interest rate does not tell the whole story. Check the YTM to know your true expected return based on today's price.
* **For tracking risk:** If a company's bond YTM suddenly shoots up very high, it means the market sees higher risk in that company.

## Quick FAQ

**Does the company pay more money if the YTM goes up?**  
No. The company continues to pay the fixed interest rate agreed upon when the bond was issued. The YTM changes only for buyers and sellers trading the bond in the secondary market.

**Why is after-tax cost of debt lower than YTM?**  
In India, interest paid on business debt is treated as an expense. This reduces the company's taxable profit, effectively saving tax and reducing the net cost of borrowing.

**When are Cost of Debt and YTM equal?**  
They are equal only when a bond is trading at its exact face value in the market, or on the day the bond is first issued at par value.
