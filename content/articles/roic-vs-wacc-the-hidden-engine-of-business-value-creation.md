Ever wondered why some companies grow rapidly but still struggle to reward their shareholders? It often comes down to a silent tug-of-war between how much a business earns and how much it costs to fund that growth.

> ## **A company creates real value only when the return it earns on its invested capital is higher than the average cost it pays to raise that capital.**

## Understanding ROIC
Return on Invested Capital (ROIC) measures how efficiently a company uses its money. Think of it as a report card for the business. If a company takes Rs 100 crore and turns it into Rs 15 crore of profit, its ROIC is 15%. This tells you how much "bang for the buck" the management team gets from every rupee they deploy.

## Understanding WACC
The Weighted Average Cost of Capital (WACC) is the hurdle rate. It represents the average cost a company pays to its lenders (interest) and its shareholders (expected returns). If a company borrows money at 10% and raises equity from investors expecting a 14% return, its WACC will be somewhere in between, say 12%. This is the "rent" the company pays to use capital.

## The Illustrative Example
Let’s look at a practical scenario. A company decides to invest Rs 100 crore into a new manufacturing plant. This project generates Rs 15 crore in annual profit. The company’s WACC is 12%.

*   **ROIC:** Rs 15 crore / Rs 100 crore = 0.15 (or 15%)
*   **WACC:** 12%

Because 15% is greater than 12%, the company is creating value. It is generating more profit than the cost of the money used to build the plant.

| Metric | Value |
| :--- | :--- |
| Invested Capital | Rs 100 crore |
| Annual Profit | Rs 15 crore |
| ROIC | 15% |
| WACC | 12% |
| Economic Value Added | Rs 3 crore |

## Calculating Economic Value Added
We can measure the exact "value added" by subtracting the cost of capital from the profit generated. In our example, the cost of capital is 12% of Rs 100 crore, which is Rs 12 crore. 

Since the project earns Rs 15 crore, the "Economic Value Added" is Rs 15 crore minus Rs 12 crore, which equals Rs 3 crore. This Rs 3 crore is the surplus value created for shareholders.

## What this means for you
*   **Growth isn't always good:** If a company grows by investing in projects where ROIC is lower than WACC, it actually destroys shareholder value.
*   **Look for the spread:** The wider the gap between ROIC and WACC, the more efficiently the company is building wealth.
*   **Sustainability matters:** A company that can maintain a high ROIC above its WACC for many years is often considered to have a strong competitive advantage.

## Quick FAQ

**Does a high ROIC always mean a company is a good investment?**
Not necessarily. While a high ROIC is excellent, you must also consider the price you pay for the stock and whether the company can sustain that high return over time.

**What happens if ROIC is lower than WACC?**
If a company’s ROIC is consistently lower than its WACC, it is essentially losing money on the capital it employs. This often leads to a decline in long-term shareholder value.

**Can WACC change over time?**
Yes. WACC changes based on interest rates in the economy, the company’s debt levels, and the perceived risk of the business. If a company takes on more debt, its WACC might change, which shifts the goalposts for value creation.

**Why is "Economic Value Added" important?**
It moves beyond simple accounting profit. It tells you if the business is actually earning enough to satisfy both the bank and the shareholders, rather than just covering basic expenses.
