Have you ever looked at a company’s profits and felt confused because they seem to jump up and down like a rollercoaster? Some businesses, like steel manufacturers or commodity producers, do not grow in a straight line. Instead, they move in cycles, riding the waves of global demand and economic health.

> ## **The core idea in one sentence**
> **To value a cyclical company, you must look beyond current profits and use a normalized average that accounts for both the highs and the lows of the business cycle.**

## Why cycles create a valuation trap

When a business is at the top of its cycle, it makes a lot of money. Because the profit is high, the Price-to-Earnings (P/E) ratio often looks very low. Many new investors see this low P/E and think the stock is a bargain. They buy it, thinking they have found a cheap deal.

However, the market knows that these high profits are temporary. Once the demand slows down, the profits will drop. When the company hits the bottom of the cycle, the profits shrink significantly. This makes the P/E ratio look very high, even if the stock price has fallen. Investors might mistakenly think the company is overvalued, when in reality, the low earnings are just a temporary phase.

## Understanding the peak and the trough

In a cyclical industry, the "peak" is the point where demand is at its highest. Prices for the company's products are high, and margins are fat. This is when the company looks like a gold mine.

The "trough" is the opposite. It is the bottom of the cycle where demand is weak. Prices are low, and the company might barely break even. This is when the company looks like a poor investment. If you judge the company only by its current state, you will almost always make the wrong decision. You need to look at the "mid-cycle" earnings. This is the average profit the company generates over several years, covering both the good times and the bad times.

## An illustrative example of normalization

Let us look at a steel company to see how this works in practice. Suppose this company earns 100 crore at the very peak of its cycle. A few years later, when the economy cools down, the same company earns only 20 crore at the trough.

If you only look at the 100 crore profit, you might overpay for the stock. If you only look at the 20 crore profit, you might miss out on a solid business. To normalize, we add these two figures together and divide by two.

*   Peak Profit: 100 crore
*   Trough Profit: 20 crore
*   Calculation: (100 + 20) / 2 = 60 crore

By using this 60 crore figure as our "sustainable annual profit," we get a much clearer picture of what the company is actually worth over the long term.

## Why normalization matters for your wallet

If you ignore the cycle, you are essentially gambling on the timing of the market. If you buy at the peak because the P/E looks low, you are likely buying when the stock is at its most expensive point. If you sell at the trough because the P/E looks high, you are likely selling when the stock is at its cheapest.

Normalization helps you avoid these emotional traps. It forces you to ask: "What is this business worth on average?" instead of "What is this business making right now?" This shift in perspective is the difference between being a reactive trader and a thoughtful, long-term investor. It allows you to build a strategy that survives the ups and downs of the Indian economy.

## What this means for you

*   Always check if the company belongs to a cyclical industry like steel, cement, or oil.
*   Look at the profit history over at least five to ten years to identify the peaks and troughs.
*   Avoid making investment decisions based on a single year of record-breaking profits.
*   Use the average profit over a full cycle to estimate a fair value for the business.
*   Stay patient, as cyclical stocks require a longer time horizon to see the full value play out.

## Quick FAQ

**Why do cyclical companies have such volatile profits?**
These companies often sell commodities where the price is set by global supply and demand. When demand is high, prices soar, but when supply exceeds demand, prices crash, dragging profits down with them.

**Is a low P/E ratio always a sign of a cheap stock?**
No. In cyclical industries, a very low P/E often appears at the peak of a cycle because earnings are inflated. This is often a warning sign that profits are about to fall, rather than a signal that the stock is cheap.

**How many years of data should I look at?**
It is best to look at a full market cycle, which typically spans 5 to 10 years. This ensures you capture at least one major boom and one major bust, giving you a realistic average of the company's earning power.

**Can I use this method for companies like software or retail?**
This method is specifically designed for cyclical businesses. Stable companies, like those in consumer goods or utilities, usually grow steadily. For them, you would use different valuation methods, as their earnings do not typically fluctuate in the same dramatic, rhythmic pattern.
