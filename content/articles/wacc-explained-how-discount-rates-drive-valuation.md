Imagine you want to start a small bakery in your neighbourhood. To buy the oven and pay rent, you use some of your own savings and borrow some money from a friend. 

Both your savings and the borrowed money come with an expectation of a return. The average cost of combining these two sources of money is the foundation of company valuation. 

In finance, this average cost is called WACC, or Weighted Average Cost of Capital.

## The core idea in one sentence

> **WACC is the average interest rate a company pays to finance its business, and analysts use it as a discount rate to figure out what the company is worth today.**

## What is WACC and how does financing work?

Every business needs money to grow. They usually get this money from two places: debt and equity. Debt is money borrowed from banks or through bonds that must be paid back with interest. Equity is money raised from investors or the owners' own pockets in exchange for a share in the business.

Debt comes with a fixed interest rate, making it easy to calculate its cost. Equity is riskier for investors, so they expect higher returns, making equity more expensive than debt.

WACC combines the cost of debt and the cost of equity based on how much of each the company uses. It represents the minimum return a company must earn on its projects to satisfy both its lenders and its shareholders.

## Why WACC acts as a discount rate

When valuing a business, analysts look at the cash the company will generate in the future. However, money in the future is worth less than money today due to risk and time. To adjust for this, future cash flows are "discounted" back to their present value.

WACC is the standard rate used for this discounting process. A higher WACC means future cash flows are worth less today because the company's cost of capital is high or risky. A lower WACC means future cash flows retain more of their value today.

When a company looks less risky, its WACC goes down. When its risk increases, its WACC goes up.

## How WACC impacts Terminal Value

Most business valuations consist of two parts: forecasted cash flows for the next few years, and the "Terminal Value." The Terminal Value estimates the worth of all cash flows beyond the forecast period into infinity.

Because the Terminal Value looks at cash flows forever, a small change in the discount rate (WACC) makes a huge difference in the final calculation.

We calculate Terminal Value using a simple formula:

*Terminal Value = Cash Flow in Next Year / (WACC - Perpetual Growth Rate)*

Let us take a business that generates an annual Free Cash Flow (FCF) of Rs 100 in its final forecast year. We assume these cash flows will grow at a steady rate (g) of 2% forever. 

Let us see what happens when the WACC changes by just 1%.

| Metric | Scenario A (Lower WACC) | Scenario B (Higher WACC) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Free Cash Flow (FCF)** | Rs 100 | Rs 100 |
| **Growth Rate (g)** | 2% | 2% |
| **WACC** | 8% | 9% |
| **WACC minus Growth Rate** | 6% (0.08 - 0.02) | 7% (0.09 - 0.02) |
| **Terminal Value Calculation** | 100 * (1 + 0.02) / 0.06 | 100 * (1 + 0.02) / 0.07 |
| **Terminal Value** | **Rs 1,700** | **Rs 1,457.14** |

## The impact of a 1% shift

In the table above, increasing the WACC from 8% to 9% caused the Terminal Value to drop from Rs 1,700 to Rs 1,457.14. 

That single percentage point change in WACC reduced the valuation by **-14.29%**.

This shows why valuation models are so sensitive to interest rates and risk perception. A small rise in borrowing costs or market risk increases the discount rate, which can erase lakhs or crores from a company's total estimated value.

## What this means for you

* **Interest rates matter:** When central banks change interest rates, WACC moves across all industries, directly impacting stock valuations.
* **Risk changes valuation:** Companies with stable profits and low debt have lower WACC, making their future cash flows worth more today.
* **Small inputs, big outcomes:** When reading valuation reports, remember that a slight tweak in the assumed discount rate can make a company look much cheaper or more expensive than it really is.

## Quick FAQ

**Why is equity usually more expensive than debt?**
Lenders get paid back first if a company fails, making debt safer. Equity investors take the ultimate risk of losing everything, so they demand a higher return to compensate for that risk.

**Does WACC stay the same forever?**
No, WACC changes whenever market interest rates change, when the company's stock risk fluctuates, or when the company changes its mix of debt and equity.

**Why is Terminal Value so sensitive to WACC?**
Terminal Value captures the value of a business forever. Because the mathematical formula divides by the difference between WACC and growth rate, a tiny shift in that gap creates a massive percentage change in the final result.
