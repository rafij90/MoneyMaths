# What Basel III Really Changed After the 2008 Crisis

In 2008, some of the world's biggest banks collapsed, or survived only because governments rescued them. The scary part was that on paper, many of these banks looked fine. They met the capital rules of the time. So what went wrong?

The answer is that the rules were too weak. They allowed banks to count low-quality items as "capital," to run on very little cash, and to borrow heavily. When trouble came, there was no real cushion. The Basel Committee on Banking Supervision (BCBS) responded with a new set of global standards called **Basel III**.

This article explains what changed, why it matters, and how to think about it as a student, investor, or bank customer.

## What exactly went wrong in 2008?

Before the crisis, three weaknesses stood out:

- **Not enough real capital.** Banks counted things as capital that could not actually absorb losses when markets fell.
- **Not enough cash.** Banks depended on short-term borrowing from other banks. When trust disappeared, that funding vanished overnight.
- **Too much debt.** Many banks, including their off-balance-sheet activities, were borrowing many times their own equity.

Basel III attacks each of these problems directly. Think of it as four big fixes.

## Fix 1: More capital, and better capital

Capital is the bank's own money, the cushion that absorbs losses before depositors or taxpayers get hurt. Basel III raised both the *amount* and the *quality* of this cushion.

**Common Equity Tier 1 (CET1)** is the best kind of capital. It mainly consists of common shares and retained profits, which can absorb losses immediately. Basel III demands that banks hold much more of it, and it phased out weaker, debt-like instruments from the core definition.

On top of the minimum, Basel III added two buffers:

- **Capital Conservation Buffer:** an extra 2.5% of risk-weighted assets. If a bank dips into this buffer, it must limit dividends and bonuses until it rebuilds.
- **Countercyclical Buffer:** an extra 0% to 2.5% that regulators can switch on when credit is growing too fast. The idea is simple: build reserves in good times so you can use them in bad times.

Here is how the numbers look for a bank with ₹10,000 crore of risk-weighted assets (RWA):

| Requirement | % of RWA | Amount |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum CET1 | 4.5% | ₹450 crore |
| Plus conservation buffer (2.5%) | 7.0% | ₹700 crore |
| Plus countercyclical buffer (if set at 1%) | 8.0% | ₹800 crore |

So the practical CET1 requirement is far higher than the headline 4.5%. Banks designated as globally or domestically systemic carry even more.

## Fix 2: Liquidity rules, so banks can survive a run

A bank can be solvent and still fail if everyone asks for their money at once. Basel III introduced two liquidity tests.

**Liquidity Coverage Ratio (LCR):** The bank must hold enough high-quality liquid assets (HQLA), such as cash and government bonds, to survive 30 days of severe stress.

> LCR = HQLA ÷ expected net cash outflows over 30 days. It must be at least 100%.

For example, if a bank expects ₹100 crore of net outflows in a stress month and holds ₹120 crore of HQLA, its LCR is 120%. It passes.

**Net Stable Funding Ratio (NSFR):** This looks at a longer, one-year horizon. It asks whether the bank's long-term assets are funded by stable, long-term sources. Before 2008, many banks funded long-term risky loans with short-term wholesale borrowing. That is like paying a 20-year home loan using a credit card you must refinance every month. The NSFR discourages exactly that.

The easy way to remember it: **LCR is about the next 30 days. NSFR is about the next 12 months.**

## Fix 3: A leverage ratio as a safety net

Risk-weighted capital rules have a weakness. They depend on how "risky" the bank *says* its assets are. If a bank underestimates risk, it can look well capitalised while borrowing heavily.

So Basel III added a simple **leverage ratio**: Tier 1 capital divided by total exposure, including off-balance-sheet items, with *no risk weighting*. The global minimum is 3%. It works as a backstop. Even if the risk models fail, a basic limit on borrowing remains.

## Fix 4: Better supervision and more disclosure

The framework rests on three pillars. Basel III strengthened two of them:

- **Pillar 2 (supervision):** Regulators got more power to review how banks measure and manage risk, and to ask for extra capital when needed.
- **Pillar 3 (disclosure):** Banks must publish more detailed information about their risk exposures and capital adequacy, so investors and analysts can judge them without relying on trust.

There was also a quieter fix. Banks' own internal models for calculating risk weights had produced wildly different results for similar assets. In the later "finalisation" phase, Basel III reduced reliance on these models and introduced an **output floor**. A bank's model-based RWA cannot fall below 72.5% of what the standardised approach would give. The goal is fair, comparable numbers across banks.

## What this means in India

India's central bank, the RBI, adopted Basel III in phases, and Indian rules are in several respects stricter than the global minimum. For example, the minimum total capital ratio is 9%, versus 8% globally. You can see these ratios in any listed bank's quarterly results. Look for the **CRAR** (capital adequacy ratio), **CET1 ratio**, **LCR**, and **leverage ratio** in the disclosures.

## What this means for you

- **If you invest in bank stocks:** Compare CET1 ratios and LCR across banks. A bank well above the minimum has room to grow loans and pay dividends. One close to the minimum may have to slow down.
- **If you are a depositor or borrower:** Stronger capital and liquidity rules make a sudden bank failure less likely. The trade-off is that holding more capital costs banks money, and some of that can show up as slightly higher lending rates.
- **If you study finance:** Remember the pattern: capital (absorb losses), liquidity (survive runs), leverage (limit debt), transparency (see the risk).

## Quick FAQ

**Is Basel III only for big global banks?**
It was designed for internationally active banks, but most countries, including India, apply it to their wider banking system, often with extra requirements for the largest banks.

**Did Basel III make banks completely safe?**
No. It lowers the odds and the damage of a crisis, but no rule can remove risk entirely. Some critics also argue that tighter rules push risky activity into less-regulated "shadow banking."

**What is the difference between Basel II and Basel III?**
Basel II focused mostly on risk-weighted capital. Basel III kept that but added higher-quality capital, buffers, liquidity ratios, and a leverage ratio.

**Where can I read the official standards?**
The Basel Committee publishes them on the Bank for International Settlements website (bis.org/committees/bcbs/standards).
